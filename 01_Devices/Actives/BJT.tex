\subsection{Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)}

A BJT is three-port, active, non-linear and time-variant device.
It is physically built from two PN junctions that share a single
region, with the two junctions oriented oppositely. This shows
up in its governing model. It is called a \emph{bipolar} device
because both electrons and holes are involved in the conduction
process.

The three terminals are the base (B), the collector (C) and the
emitter (E). It is often called a \emph{current controlled device}
because of its base-collector current relationship. In reality, the
base-emitter voltage controls the collector current.

BJT family has two types of transistors: NPN and PNP.\ They are
(in a sense) mirror images of each other.

The behaviour of a BJT can be approximated well by the below equations.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Current \& Relations}:
    The currents flowing into and out of the terminals of a BJT are
    related:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Collector Current (\(I_C\))}:
        The Ebers-Moll Model approximately describe the collector current as
        two coupled Shockley's diode equations:
        \[
          I_C = \underbrace{\alpha_F I_{FS} \left( e^{\frac{V_{BE}}{V_T}} - 1 \right)}_{\text{Forward Junction Contribution}} - \underbrace{\alpha_R I_{RS} \left( e^{\frac{V_{BC}}{V_T}} - 1 \right)}_{\text{Reverse Junction Contribution}}
        \]
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \(I_C\) is the collector current
          \item \(I_{FS}\) is the forward saturation current
          \item \(\alpha_F\) is the forward common base current gain
          \item \(V_{BE}\) is the base-emitter voltage
          \item \(V_T\) is the thermal voltage
          \item \(I_{RS}\) is the reverse saturation current
          \item \(\alpha_R\) is the reverse common base current gain
          \item \(V_{BC}\) is the base-collector voltage
        \end{itemize}
        In forward active mode, the base-collector voltage is reverse biased
        (\(V_{BC} \ll 0\)) and thus \( e^{\frac{V_{BC}}{V_T}} \approx 0 \) and the
        Ebers-Moll model simplifies,
        \begin{align*}
          I_C &= \alpha_F I_{FS} \left( e^{\frac{V_{BE}}{V_T}} - 1 \right) - \alpha_R I_{RS} \left( e^{\frac{V_{BC}}{V_T}} - 1 \right) \\
          I_C &= \alpha_F I_{FS} \left( e^{\frac{V_{BE}}{V_T}} - 1 \right) - \alpha_R I_{RS}(-1) \\
          I_C &= \alpha_F I_{FS} \left( e^{\frac{V_{BE}}{V_T}} - 1 \right) + \alpha_R I_{RS} \\
          I_C &\approx \alpha_F I_{FS} \left( e^{\frac{V_{BE}}{V_T}} - 1 \right)
        \end{align*}
        Forward Saturation Current (\(I_{FS}\)) and Reverse Saturation Current (\(I_{RS}\))
        can be related to each other by defining,
        \[
          \alpha_F I_{FS} = \alpha_R I_{RS} = I_S
        \]
        We'll use \(I_S\) as the saturation current in the rest of this document.
      \item \textbf{Base Current (\(I_B\))}:
        The base current is related to the collector current by the current gain
        (\(\beta\)):
        \[
          I_B = \frac{I_C}{\beta}
        \]
        \(\beta\) is not constant and varies with temperature, collector current and
        collector-emitter voltage. Use Ebers-Moll model instead.
      \item \textbf{Emitter Current (\(I_E\))}:
        The emitter current is the sum of the collector and base currents:
        \begin{align*}
          I_E &= I_C + I_B \\
          I_E &= I_C + \frac{I_C}{\beta} \\
          I_E &= I_C \left( 1 + \frac{1}{\beta} \right)
        \end{align*}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Voltages \& Relations}:
    There are several important voltages in a BJT:\
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Thermal/Boltzmann Voltage (\(V_T\))}:
        The thermal voltage is given by:
        \[
          V_T = \frac{kT}{q}
        \]
        where \(k\) is the Boltzmann constant, \(T\) is the temperature in
        Kelvin and \(q\) is the charge of an electron. At room temperature,
        \(V_T \approx 26\,\mathrm{mV}\):
      \item \textbf{Threshold Voltage (\(V_{th}\))}:
        The threshold voltage is the minimum voltage required to bias a junction
        such that it conducts. For a silicon junction (most common), the
        threshold voltage is \(V_{th} \approx 0.6\text{--}0.7\,\mathrm{V}\).
        Since we have two junctions in a BJT, we have two threshold voltages.
        We define \(V_{Fth}\) for the forward junction threshold voltage and
        \(V_{Rth}\) for the reverse junction threshold voltage.
    \end{itemize}
    They are also related to each other:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Base-Emitter \(V_{BE}\) and Base-Collector \(V_{BC}\) Voltages}:
        If you rearranged Ebers-Moll model for \(V_{BE}\) or \(V_{BC}\) after
        simplifying for forward active or reverse active mode, you get:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Under Forward Active Mode}:
            \begin{align*}
              V_{BE} = V_T \ln \left( \frac{I_C}{I_{S}} + 1 \right) \\
              V_{BC} \ll 0
            \end{align*}
          \item \textbf{Under Reverse Active Mode}:
            \begin{align*}
              V_{BE} \ll 0 \\
              V_{BC} = V_T \ln \left( \frac{I_C}{I_{S}} + 1 \right)
            \end{align*}
          \end{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Collector-Emitter \(V_{CE}\) Voltage}:
        The collector-emitter voltage is the voltage across the collector and
        emitter terminals. It is given by:
        \begin{align*}
          V_{CE} &= V_{BE} - V_{BC} \\
          V_{CE} &= V_B - V_E - (V_B - V_C) \\
          V_{CE} &= V_B - V_E - V_B + V_C \\
          V_{CE} &= V_C - V_E
        \end{align*}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Modes of Operation}:
    A BJT can operate in four different modes depending on the biasing of
    the base-emitter and base-collector junctions. Lets assume
    \(V_{Fth} \approx 0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(V_{Rth} \approx -0.7\,\mathrm{V}\)
    for a silicon BJT.\
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|X|X|X|X|} \hline
      Mode & Base-Emitter Voltage & Base-Collector Voltage & Collector Current \\ \hline
      Cutoff                     & $V_{BE} < 0.7       $ & $V_{BC} < -0.7 $ & $I_C \approx 0$ \\ \hline
      Forward Active (Amplifier) & $V_{BE} \gtrsim 0.7 $ & $V_{BC} < -0.7 $ & $I_C \approx \beta I_B$ \\ \hline
      Saturation (Switch)        & $V_{BE} \gtrsim 0.7 $ & $V_{BC} \gtrsim -0.7 $ & $I_C \approx I_{C(sat)}$ \\ \hline
      Reverse Active             & $V_{BE} < 0.7       $ & $V_{BC} \gtrsim -0.7 $ & $I_C \approx \beta_R I_B$ \\ \hline
    \end{tabularx}\\
    Here, \(\beta_R\) is the reverse current gain, which is typically much
    smaller than \(\beta\) and is often ignored in practice.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Saturation and Cutoff (Switching)}:
        To use a BJT as a switch, the device is deliberately driven between
        only two of its four operating modes: \emph{cutoff} (off) and
        \emph{saturation} (on). Consider an NPN with its emitter grounded,
        its collector connected to the load and \(V_{CC}\) through the load
        resistance \(R_C\), and its base driven through a series resistor
        \(R_B\) from a logic-level input \(V_{IN}\).
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Off State}:
            When \(V_{IN} < 0.7\), the base-emitter junction does not
            conduct, so \(I_B \approx 0\) and \(I_C \approx 0\) (ignoring
            leakage current). The transistor is in cutoff and the full
            supply voltage appears across the collector-emitter terminals,
            \[
              V_{CE} \approx V_{CC}
            \]
          \item \textbf{On State (Saturation)}:
            When driven on, we want enough collector current such that entire
            \(V_{cc}\) is dropped across \(R_C\) which means \(V_{CE}\) collapses
            to \(V_{CE(sat)}\), typically \(0.1\text{--}0.3\,\mathrm{V}\)
            for a small-signal device. The collector current is then set
            almost entirely by the external circuit (represented as \(R_C\)),
            not by the transistor:
            \[
              I_{C(sat)} = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{CE(sat)}}{R_C}
            \]
            In saturation, \(I_C\) is no longer \(\beta I_B\). It is
            load-limited and \(\beta\) only tells us the minimum base
            current required to reach this state.
          \item \textbf{Sizing the Base Resistor}:
            The edge of saturation occurs when \(I_B = I_{C(sat)}/\beta\).
            Because \(\beta\) varies with temperature, manufacturing
            tolerance and collector current itself (and its datasheet
            minimum can be several times lower than its typical value),
            driving the base at exactly this current is not robust. Instead
            we define a \emph{forced beta} \(\beta_{forced}\), deliberately
            chosen much lower than the transistor's minimum guaranteed
            \(\beta\) (a common choice is \(\beta_{forced} \approx 10\)),
            and size the base drive to guarantee saturation under worst-case
            conditions:
            \[
              I_B = \frac{I_{C(sat)}}{\beta_{forced}}
            \]
            Applying KVL around the base loop, with \(V_{BE(sat)} \approx
            0.7\text{--}0.8\,\mathrm{V}\),
            \begin{align*}
              V_{IN} &= I_B R_B + V_{BE(sat)} \\
              R_B &= \frac{V_{IN} - V_{BE(sat)}}{I_B}
            \end{align*}
            Choosing a smaller \(R_B\) than this (i.e.\ more base overdrive)
            pushes the transistor deeper into saturation, at the cost of
            wasted base drive current and, for BJTs specifically, increased
            \emph{storage time} during turn-off (excess minority carriers
            stored in the base must first be removed before \(I_C\) can
            begin to fall).
          \item \textbf{Inductive Loads}:
            If \(R_C\) is replaced by an inductive load (a relay coil or
            motor winding), the collapsing magnetic field on turn-off
            induces a large negative voltage spike across the collector,
            since the inductor resists the sudden change in current. This
            can exceed the transistor's \(V_{CE(\text{max})}\) rating and destroy
            it. A \textbf{flyback (freewheeling) diode} placed in reverse
            bias across the load gives the inductor's current a path to
            decay through, clamping the spike to roughly one diode drop
            above \(V_{CC}\).
        \end{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Forward Active (Amplification)}:
        To use a BJT as an amplifier, it must be held in forward active
        mode at all times, including for the full swing of the AC signal
        superimposed on it. This requires establishing a stable DC
        \emph{operating point} (or \emph{Q-point}), specified by a
        quiescent \(I_C\) and \(V_{CE}\), before any AC analysis is
        meaningful.
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{The Beta-Sensitivity Problem}:
            A naive bias scheme (a single resistor from \(V_{CC}\) to the
            base) sets \(I_B\) directly, and therefore sets \(I_C =
            \beta I_B\). Since \(\beta\) is not a controlled or stable
            parameter, the Q-point of such a circuit shifts significantly
            between individual transistors and with temperature. A robust
            bias network must set \(I_C\) largely \emph{independently} of
            \(\beta\).
          \item \textbf{Voltage Divider Bias}:
            The standard solution adds a resistive divider (\(R_1\),
            \(R_2\)) from \(V_{CC}\) to ground to fix the base voltage, and
            an emitter resistor \(R_E\) to provide negative feedback: any
            increase in \(I_C\) increases \(I_E R_E\), which reduces
            \(V_{BE}\) and pulls \(I_C\) back down.

            Applying Thevenin's theorem to the divider as seen from the
            base,
            \begin{align*}
              V_{TH} &= V_{CC} \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \\
              R_{TH} &= R_1 \parallel R_2
            \end{align*}
            Writing KVL around the base-emitter loop, and using
            \(I_E = I_C \left(1 + \frac{1}{\beta}\right)\) and
            \(I_B = I_C/\beta\) as derived earlier,
            \begin{align*}
              V_{TH} &= I_B R_{TH} + V_{BE} + I_E R_E \\
              V_{TH} &= \frac{I_C}{\beta} R_{TH} + V_{BE} + I_C \left(1 + \frac{1}{\beta}\right) R_E
            \end{align*}
            Solving for \(I_C\),
            \[
              I_C = \frac{V_{TH} - V_{BE}}{R_E + \dfrac{R_{TH}}{\beta} + \dfrac{R_E}{\beta}}
            \]
            This expression makes the design condition explicit: if the
            divider is stiff enough that \(R_{TH} \ll \beta R_E\), the
            \(\beta\)-dependent terms in the denominator become negligible
            and
            \[
              I_C \approx \frac{V_{TH} - V_{BE}}{R_E}
            \]
            which depends only on the divider ratio, \(V_{BE}\) (itself
            weakly dependent on temperature and current, per the earlier
            forward-active relation), and \(R_E\) — not on \(\beta\) at
            all. In practice this is enforced with a rule of thumb: choose
            the divider current \(I_{div} = V_{CC}/(R_1+R_2)\) to be roughly
            an order of magnitude larger than the expected base current
            \(I_B\), so the base current drawn from the divider node
            perturbs \(V_{TH}\) only slightly.
          \item \textbf{Setting the Collector Voltage / Load Line}:
            With \(I_C\) fixed by the emitter-degenerated bias above, the
            collector resistor \(R_C\) is chosen to place \(V_{CE}\) at the
            desired quiescent point, typically near the middle of the
            available voltage swing so the output can swing symmetrically
            in both directions before clipping,
            \[
              V_{CC} = I_C R_C + V_{CE} + I_E R_E \approx I_C(R_C + R_E) + V_{CE}
            \]
            A common design target is \(V_{CE} \approx V_{CC}/2\).
          \item \textbf{Small-Signal AC Gain}:
            Once the Q-point is fixed, small AC signals superimposed on the
            DC bias see the transistor as a locally linear device,
            characterised by its transconductance and input resistance at
            that operating point,
            \begin{align*}
              g_m &= \frac{I_C}{V_T} \\
              r_\pi &= \frac{\beta}{g_m} = \frac{\beta V_T}{I_C}
            \end{align*}
            For a common-emitter stage with the emitter resistor \(R_E\)
            AC-bypassed by a capacitor (so \(R_E\) sets the DC bias point
            but is shorted at signal frequencies), the small-signal
            voltage gain from base to collector is
            \[
              A_v = -g_m R_C = -\frac{I_C R_C}{V_T}
            \]
            The negative sign indicates the characteristic \(180^\circ\)
            phase inversion of the common-emitter configuration. If
            \(R_E\) is left unbypassed, it appears in series and reduces
            (but stabilises) the gain,
            \[
              A_v \approx -\frac{R_C}{R_E} \hspace{1cm} \text{[for } g_m R_E \gg 1\text{]}
            \]
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Effects}:
    BJTs are affected by many non-linear effects that are not captured by
    the Ebers-Moll model. Sometimes they cause hard to diagnose problems.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Base Width Modulation}: Increasing \(V_{CE}\) shrinks the base
          width physically. This increases the collector current and is captured
          by the Early voltage (\(V_A\)) and produces the Early Effect.
          \[
            I_C = I_{S} \left( e^{\frac{V_{BE}}{V_T}} - 1 \right) \left( 1 + \frac{V_{CE}}{V_A} \right) \hspace{1cm} \text{[forward active]}
          \]
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Noise}:
      BJTs are also affected by noise namely, thermal noise, shot noise and flicker
      noise. You can read more about these noises in~\ref{sec:theory_general_noise}.
\end{itemize}
