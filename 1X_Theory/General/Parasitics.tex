\subsection{Parasitics}
\label{sec:theory_general_parasitics}


In real-world circuits, components are not ideal and exhibit
parasitic characteristics that can affect circuit performance.
You can safely assume everything has certain parasitics and
ignoring them can lead to inaccurate results especially in
high-frequency or precision applications. You can almost ignore
them in low-frequency and moderate-precision applications.

At high frequencies, Everything can be modeled as a combination
of resistors, capacitors, and inductors.

\subsubsection{Types of Parasitics}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Parasitic Capacitance:} Two or more than two
    conductors that are close to each other can form a
    capacitor, even if they are not intended to do so. This
    severely affects high-frequency signals.
  \item \textbf{Parasitic Inductance:} All conductive materials
    have some inherent inductance due to the magnetic fields
    generated by current flow. This can lead to unwanted
    voltage spikes and signal distortion, especially at high
    frequencies.
  \item \textbf{Parasitic Resistance:} All materials have some
    inherent resistance to current flow. This can lead to
    power loss and heating effects in circuits.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Resistor Parasitic}
Below I've shown the model of a real resistor including its
parasitic elements whose ideal resistance should've been just
\(R_2\).
\begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    % Paths, nodes and wires:
	  \draw (8.75, 7) to[european resistor, l={$R_2$}] (10.5, 7);
	  \draw (8.75, 7) to[european inductor, l_={$L_2$}] (7, 7);
	  \draw (4.5, 7) to[european resistor, l={$R_1$}] (6.25, 7);
	  \draw (4.5, 7) to[european inductor, l_={$L_1$}] (2.75, 7);
	  \draw (13, 7) to[european resistor, l={$R_3$}] (14.75, 7);
	  \draw (13, 7) to[european inductor, l_={$L_3$}] (11.25, 7);
	  \draw (8, 8.75) to[capacitor, l={$C_{13}$}] (9.5, 8.75);
	  \draw (8, 8.75) -- (2.75, 8.75) -- (2.75, 7);
	  \draw (6.25, 7) -- (7, 7);

The total impedance of the resistor can be calculated as:
\begin{align*}
  Z_{\text{resistor}} &= (X_{L_1} + R_1 + X_{L_2} + R_2 + X_{L_3} + R_3) \parallel X_{C_{13}} \\
                      &= ( j\omega L_1 + R_1 + j\omega L_2 + R_2 + j\omega L_3 + R_3) \parallel \frac{1}{j\omega C_{13}} \\
                      &= (R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + j\omega (L_1 + L_2 + L_3)) \parallel \frac{1}{j\omega C_{13}} \\
\end{align*}

You can clearly see that even something as simple as a resistor
isn't truly just a resistor anymore when you consider its
parasitic elements.
